Four lines. That's all it takes to protect your constitutional rights during a traffic stop. Say the wrong thing — or say too much — and you could be handing a prosecutor the evidence they need. Say these four lines, and you stay in control.

Most people lose their rights during traffic stops not because they're guilty, but because they don't know what to say — and they talk too much in a moment where every word becomes evidence.

Here are the four lines, refined over decades of civil-rights litigation.

Line one: I do not consent to any searches.
Line two: Am I being detained, or am I free to go?
Line three: I am exercising my right to remain silent.
Line four: I would like to speak to a lawyer.

That's it. Four lines. Memorize them. Practice them in the mirror until they feel boring. Because in the moment, you will not invent good wording — you will revert to whatever you've rehearsed.

Now here's something that surprises most people. A routine traffic stop is NOT considered custody for Miranda purposes. That means officers can ask you questions at the roadside — 'Do you know why I pulled you over? Have you been drinking? Where are you headed?' — without reading you your rights. And your answers are still admissible in court.

That question — 'Do you know why I pulled you over?' — is a trap. It's designed to get an admission. No matter how you answer, you're giving them evidence.

The Innocence Project has documented over 375 DNA exonerations. Roughly 30% involved false confessions or statements from people who thought talking would clear them. Innocent people. Convicted because they said too much.

So what does this all mean?

It means the roadside is not where you win. Court is. Your job during a traffic stop is not to prove your innocence — it's to preserve your ability to fight later.

Police can legally lie to you about evidence. They can claim your friend is in the next room confessing. They can say they already have fingerprints. None of it has to be true. The only defense is to say nothing that depends on what they tell you.

And here's the part most people miss: you must verbally invoke your rights. The Supreme Court held in Salinas v. Texas that silence alone can be used against you. You have to say the words. Out loud. Clearly. Every time.

Four lines. Memorize them. Practice them. Because the next time those red and blue lights appear in your rearview mirror, what you say in the next sixty seconds could change everything.
