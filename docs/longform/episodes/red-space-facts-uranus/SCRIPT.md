It took until 1850 for Uranus to become the name everyone used. | timeline | WU | History of the Universe: the human story inside the science |
| S06 | a planet nobody saw | Within two years astronomers accepted it as a new planet. It is big: four times Earth's width and more than fourteen times its mass. | S07b | the only visit | Getting there at all needed luck with geometry. In the late 1970s the outer planets lined up in a way that let one spacecraft use each planet's gravity to swing on to the next. NASA began planning a grand tour, and Voyager 2 became the only probe to fly that full route: Jupiter, Saturn, then Uranus, and finally Neptune, which it passed on the twenty-fifth of August 1989. | timeline | WV | The Space Race: mission explained as a dated sequence |
| S08 | the only visit | Voyager 2 launched on the twentieth of August 1977, using a rare alignment of the outer planets to swing from world to world. It reached Uranus on the twenty-fourth of January 1986 and passed about eighty-one thousand kilometres above the clouds. What it saw was almost featureless: across the whole planet it counted just ten cloud features. | pair | WV, IMG | Astrum: real Voyager image panels side by side with column titles |
| S08b | the only visit | The rings had been found nine years earlier, by accident. On the tenth of March 1977, astronomers flying on NASA's Kuiper Airborne Observatory planned to study the planet's atmosphere by watching a star pass behind it. Before and after the planet covered the star, the star briefly disappeared five times. The only explanation was a set of narrow rings. Voyager confirmed them and found two more. | image · 1986 | WR | Real Voyager ring image, labelled |
| S09 | the only visit | The real surprise was invisible. Uranus's magnetic field turned out to be tilted about fifty-nine degrees away from its spin axis and shifted far off the planet's centre. | S09b | the only visit | Voyager's data is still giving up secrets. It was in the data for thirty-four years before anyone noticed. | image | WU, SVS | SVS visualisation frame, labelled as a visualisation |
| S10 | the only visit | Voyager also arrived at an awkward moment. It came during the height of the southern summer, with the south pole facing the Sun, so it could not see the northern hemisphere at all. Half of the planet stayed hidden until telescopes on and near Earth could watch the seasons turn. | seasons | NF, WV | Astrum: real image with labels |
| S10b | the only visit | And the planet that looked so calm in 1986 turned out to have weather. From March to May 2004, large clouds appeared and observers measured winds of up to eight hundred and twenty kilometres an hour. The seasons were changing the sky. | image · 2023 | WU, IMG | Real Webb image with labels; weather as a dated observation |
| S11 | why it tipped over | So why is Uranus on its side? Nobody knows for certain. The idea most often discussed is that something roughly the size of Earth hit it early in its history and knocked it over. That is a hypothesis, not an observation. It is also used to explain another oddity: Uranus gives off almost no heat of its own. | planet | WU | Our model scene, marked HYPOTHESIS |
| S12 | why it tipped over | Neptune, its near twin in size, radiates more than two and a half times as much energy as it gets from the Sun. Uranus radiates about the same amount it receives, and its tropopause has been measured at forty-nine kelvin, the coldest temperature recorded on any planet. Why its interior is so quiet is still not understood. | facts | WU | Astrum: data card |
| S13 | rings and moons | Uranus also has rings: thirteen of them, narrow and dark. | S14 | rings and moons | Its moons are named after characters from Shakespeare and Alexander Pope: Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, Oberon.
