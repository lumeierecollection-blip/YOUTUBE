| P01 | cold open | Imagine you are a teacher. Before term starts, a researcher hands you a list of names. These children, he says, scored high on a new test that spots pupils about to bloom intellectually. Expect big things from them this year. What you are not told is that the names were picked at random. | scenario | WP | PsychToons CkP_8bkjf1E: second-person scenario opening |
| P02 | cold open | This is the idea behind the Pygmalion effect: that what we expect of someone changes how we treat them, how we treat them changes what they believe about themselves, and in the end they perform the way we expected. It is one of the most repeated ideas in education and management. The question is how much of it is true. | cycle | WP | Sprouts 4aN5TbGW5JA: the effect as a cycle of people and arrows |
| P03 | the horse | The idea has a strange ancestor: a horse. In early twentieth-century Germany, Clever Hans appeared to do arithmetic, tapping out answers with a hoof. In 1907 the psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed the horse was not counting. It was reading tiny, unconscious cues in the body language of the people asking the questions, who had no idea they were giving the answer away. | study | CH, WP | Practical Psychology: a historical case before the experiment |
| P03b | the horse | Hans belonged to Wilhelm von Osten, a mathematics teacher who believed his horse could add, divide and tell the time. That is what made the case so important to psychologists: an observer could change the result of a test without meaning to and without knowing it. Decades later the sociologist Robert Merton named a related idea: the self-fulfilling prophecy, a false belief that makes itself come true. | study | CH, JH | Sprouts: the person behind the idea |
| P04 | the experiment | If expectations could leak out of a questioner into a horse, could they leak from a teacher into a child? In the 1960s the psychologist Robert Rosenthal, then at Harvard, and his co-author Lenore Jacobson set out to test it in a single elementary school in California. | study | RJ, WP, COMMONS | Sprouts: drawn portrait of the researcher; our study card |
| P05 | the experiment | Every pupil took an IQ test disguised as a test of future academic growth. The scores were never shown to the teachers. Instead, about one in five pupils in each class was picked at random and named as a bloomer. At the end of the year, everyone took the same test again. | experiment | WP, RJ | Practical Psychology -W-ML_dG_4Y: the procedure step by step |
| P06 | the result | In the first grade the result was dramatic. The children labelled as bloomers out-gained their classmates by about fifteen IQ points. In the second grade the gap was about ten points. Nothing about those children had been different, except the label in their teacher's head. | stat | JH | Practical Psychology: one large number per finding |
| P07 | the result | That is the part of the story most people hear. But the same study had six grades. In the third grade there was no difference at all. In the fourth it was too small to be meaningful. In the fifth and sixth grades the bloomers actually gained slightly less than the other children. | experiment | JH | Our experiment reconstruction, results panel |
| P08 | the result | Averaged across the whole school, the difference between bloomers and the rest was about four IQ points. In the language of statistics that is an effect size of about point three, which researchers normally call small. And in a second year of follow-up, significant effects showed up in only one of five grades. Across the eleven grade-years examined, eight showed no self-fulfilling prophecy at all. | stat | JH | Practical Psychology: the number restated in plain terms |
| P08b | the result | There is also a question the study never asked, for good reason. Rosenthal and Jacobson only ever planted positive expectations. Telling teachers that some children were about to fall behind would have been unethical. So the study says nothing about whether low expectations drag children down, which is the version of the idea people worry about most. | claims | JH | Our claim card: what the study could not test |
| P09 | the critics | Critics went further. The educational psychologist Robert Thorndike looked at the test the study used and found nonsense in it: one ordinary class had an average reasoning score in the range of intellectual disability. A rise from scores like that, he argued, is just as likely to be a statistical bounce back to normal as an effect of anyone's expectations. His verdict on the test: when the clock strikes thirteen, you doubt every stroke that came before it. | study | WPC | SunnyV2/Court technique applied to science: the critic quoted on a card |
| P10 | the replications | So other researchers tried to repeat it. By the early 1980s there were eighteen experiments like it. Stephen Raudenbush gathered them into one analysis. Each dot here is one result. Most sit close to zero. The original study is the highlighted dot, and it is one of the larger results. | effect | R84 | Our data scene: every study as a dot (real meta-analysis data) |
| P11 | the replications | Pooled together, the average effect is tiny: about point zero eight, and not reliably different from zero. But Raudenbush spotted a pattern. When the teachers had known the children for more than two weeks before being given the expectation, the effect was close to nothing. It only appeared when the label arrived first, before the teacher had formed an impression of their own. | effect | R84, WPC | Same data scene, the pooled mean drawn and the moderator highlighted |
| P11b | the replications | The repeat studies found something else that matters. When researchers measured outcomes like test scores, most found no advantage for the children labelled as bloomers. But when they watched what happened in the classroom, most found that teachers did treat those children more favourably. The expectation changed the teacher. It just did not reliably change the child's IQ score. | scenario | WPC | Our scenario: process versus outcome |
| P12 | the replications | In 2005 Lee Jussim and Kent Harber reviewed thirty-five years of this research. Their conclusion sits between the extremes. Self-fulfilling prophecies in classrooms do happen. But the effects are usually small, they do not pile up over time, and they are more likely to fade than to grow. | claims | JH | Our claim-vs-evidence layout |
| P13 | the twist | There is one more twist. If teachers' expectations predict how children do, that might not be because the expectations cause it. It might be because teachers are mostly right. Jussim and Harber concluded that teacher expectations may predict student outcomes more because they are accurate than because they are self-fulfilling. | stat | JH | Our data-led reveal |
| P14 | beyond the classroom | The idea did travel. In the Israeli Defence Forces in 1982, researchers named Eden and Shani told instructors that some randomly chosen trainees had exceptional potential, and those trainees outperformed the rest on four objective tests. Studies in workplaces and a nursing home followed. But researchers have also found the effect harder to produce with women in some studies, and deliberately deceiving managers raises its own ethical problems. | scenario | WP | Sprouts: the idea applied to work; marked with its limits |
| P15 | ending | So does believing in someone make them smarter? The honest answer: a little, sometimes, mostly when you have not met them yet, and not for long. The famous version of the Pygmalion effect is bigger than the evidence. The real one is smaller and stranger. Expectations matter most when we know the least. | cycle | WP, JH, R84 | Back to the cycle, the weak link marked |
