| N03 | what a master is | Under American copyright law, a recorded song is really two things. There is the composition: the melody and the lyrics, which belong to whoever wrote them. And there is the master: the specific recording you hear on the album. Whoever owns the master controls every copy of that recording, from streams to vinyl, and has to license it whenever someone wants to use it. | explainer | TM | VOX explainer: the term defined, then the two parts drawn |
| N04 | how she lost them | In 2004 a record executive, Scott Borchetta, saw a teenage Swift play at Nashville's Bluebird Café. She became the first artist on his new label, Big Machine, on a thirteen-year deal that gave the label ownership of her first six albums' masters in exchange for a cash advance. Between 2006 and 2017 she released six albums with it: Taylor Swift, Fearless, Speak Now, Red, 1989 and Reputation. Big Machine owned the masters, as labels usually do. Swift, who wrote her songs, kept the publishing rights. | compare | TM, COMMONS | Magazine compare: two eras, real photographs |
| N05 | how she lost them | In August 2018, with her contract nearly over, Swift's lawyer proposed that the masters be sold back to her. According to Billboard, the label said that would only happen if she signed on again and made more albums for the next decade. They never agreed. That November she left for Republic Records, part of Universal Music Group, on a deal under which she would own the masters of her new albums. | flow | TM | VOX ownership diagram |
| N06 | the sale | Then, in June 2019, the Wall Street Journal reported that the music manager Scooter Braun's company, Ithaca Holdings, had bought Big Machine for an estimated three hundred and thirty million dollars, financed by private equity firms. With the label came everything it owned, including Swift's first six albums. | flow | TM | VOX money flow: the price on the arrow |
| N07 | the sale | Swift posted her response on Tumblr the same day the sale was announced. She said she had asked for years to buy her work, and had only been offered a deal that required six more albums. She called Braun an incessant, manipulative bully. That is her description of him, not a finding by anyone else, and Big Machine disputed her account. | post · 30 June 2019 | TM | Patrick Cc: the public post as the evidence, a contested description marked as one side's view |
| N08 | the sale | Big Machine's founder, Scott Borchetta, answered with a blog post titled "It's Time for Some Truth". Each side has kept its own account of what was said, and when. | quote | TM | SunnyV2 quote card: the other side, in its own words |
| N08b | the fight | In November 2019 it escalated. Swift said Braun and Borchetta were blocking her from performing her older songs at the American Music Awards, where she was to be named Artist of the Decade, and from using old recordings in her Netflix documentary. She said the message was: be a good little girl and shut up. Big Machine rejected her claim and accused her of enlisting her fans. Days later it agreed to license its artists' performances for the broadcast, without naming her. | post · 14 Nov 2019 | TM, COMMONS | SWOOP: the claim, then the other side's statement; status chips keep them apart |
| N08c | the fight | Braun later said Swift had weaponised her fans, and that he and his family had received death threats. Then, in April 2020, Big Machine released an old radio performance of hers, Live from Clear Channel Stripped 2008. Swift said she had not authorised it and called it shameless greed in the time of coronavirus. It sold thirty-three copies in the US and never charted. | record | TM | Two sides, two cards (our investigation treatment) |
| N09 | the plan | Swift had an unusual way out. US copyright law says the rights in a recording do not stop anyone making a brand-new recording of the same song. Because she owned the songs themselves, she could record them again, as close to the originals as she liked, once the restriction in her old contract ran out. In August 2019 she said on television that this was exactly what she would do. | explainer | TM, US | VOX explainer: the rule that made the plan possible |
| N10 | the plan | In October 2020 Braun sold the masters on, to Shamrock Holdings, a private equity firm owned by the estate of Roy E. Disney, for a reported four hundred and five million dollars, with Braun still set to profit from them. Swift said Braun would only let her bid if she signed a non-disclosure agreement she called ironclad. She refused, and she turned down an offer to become an equity partner with Shamrock, because Braun would keep benefiting. In November 2020 she started re-recording. | flow | TM | VOX money flow, second sale |
| N11 | taylor's version | In April 2021 she released Fearless (Taylor's Version). It went straight to number one in the US. The original Fearless, which had still been on the Billboard 200 at number one hundred and fifty-seven, dropped out of the chart altogether as its sales fell by nineteen percent. | record | TM | Magazine record card: the achievement, measured |
| N12 | taylor's version | Red (Taylor's Version) followed in November 2021 with all thirty songs she had written for the original. Then Speak Now in 2023, and 1989 (Taylor's Version) that October, which put its songs in the top six places of the Billboard Global 200 at the same time, the first time any artist had done that. | bars | TM | Bars: the re-recordings in order |
| N13 | the industry | The rest of the industry noticed. In November 2021 the Wall Street Journal reported that Universal had doubled the time its new contracts bar artists from re-recording. By 2023, according to Billboard, the major labels were asking for waits of between ten and thirty years. Other artists, from Olivia Rodrigo to Ashanti, said Swift's fight changed how they handled their own masters. | record | TM | VOX: consequence beyond the person |
| N14 | the ending | The last sale was between Swift and the investors. Shamrock, which had held the recordings since 2020, offered her what she called fairer terms. The price was not disclosed. Billboard's sources put it at nearly three hundred and sixty million dollars, so treat that as reported, not confirmed. She credited the success of the re-recordings for making the deal possible. | flow | TM, BB | VOX money flow, final sale |
| N15 | the ending | The day the news broke, listening to her original albums jumped, according to figures Spotify shared with the Hollywood Reporter.
