In 1666, a single spark in a bakery destroyed 13,000 homes, 87 churches, and most of London. But the real story isn't about fire. It's about human failure.

London in 1666 was a city waiting to burn. Timber-framed buildings pressed shoulder to shoulder along medieval streets. The summer had been scorching and bone-dry. The wood was seasoned to perfection for burning.

Thomas Farriner's bakery on Pudding Lane was the epicenter. His oven was allegedly left unattended on Saturday night. By dawn on Sunday, September 2nd, flames had already taken hold.

But here's what most people don't know: the fire could have been stopped. The Lord Mayor of London, Sir Thomas Bloodworth, was woken with the news. His response? 'A woman could piss it out.' He refused to demolish surrounding buildings to create firebreaks.

By the time he changed his mind, it was too late. The fire had grown beyond control.

Samuel Pepys, a diarist and naval administrator, watched the destruction unfold. His account is one of the most vivid we have.

'I saw a fire as one entire arch of fire above a mile long: it made me weep to see it. The churches, houses are all on fire and flaming at once, and a horrid noise the flames made and the cracking of the houses.'

Pepys made a decision that sounds bizarre today: he buried his Parmesan cheese and wine in his garden. But in 1666, this was practical crisis management. Hard cheese was imported, expensive, and remarkably durable. With London descending into chaos, Pepys was safeguarding a compact store of wealth and calories.

The fire raged for four days. It consumed everything in its path: 13,200 houses, 87 churches, most of the City of London. Only six deaths were officially recorded. But historians believe the true number was much higher. The poor, the homeless, the undocumented—they simply vanished.

The fire reached the Thames and stopped there. Water on one side, open ground on the other. The city was saved from complete annihilation.

In the aftermath, three plans were presented to King Charles II for rebuilding the city. Christopher Wren, John Evelyn, and Robert Hooke each had a vision. None of their plans were fully adopted. The old street lines were retained.

But Wren's masterpiece survived: St. Paul's Cathedral, rebuilt from the ashes, still stands today. The fire had swept away the medieval city—with all its inefficiency, its fire hazard, its disease-prone density—and created space for something new.

The London that emerged was more orderly, more regulated, more ambitious. The fire didn't destroy London. It transformed it.

When you walk through London today, you're walking through a city that rose from ashes. Every brick tells that story. The Great Fire of London wasn't just a disaster. It was the moment a city learned to rebuild.
